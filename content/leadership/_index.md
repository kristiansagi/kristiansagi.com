#### _What I've learned about building products and the organizations behind them, mostly by getting it wrong first._

---

## People

### People first
A title makes you a manager. The people you work with decide whether you're a leader. My job is to help people do the best work of their careers. That means knowing them as whole people, including their family, their health and what they want next, because people do their best work for someone who cares about them, not just about their output.

### Trust comes first
Nothing else on this page works without trust. People need to know I'll keep my word, keep their confidences and back them when it counts. When a project fails, that's on me, not the team. Owning it is how trust gets built.

---

## Product

### Make the right product before making the product right
Polishing the wrong thing is the most expensive mistake a team can make. Before we optimize anything, I want to be sure we're solving a real problem for a real customer. Once we are, we hold nothing back on quality. Engineering, design and product decide this together as one team, and the best argument wins, whatever the title.

### Focus means saying no
Every good team has more good ideas than it can execute. My job is to protect a few great ones from many good ones. Deleting is as important as adding, whether it's features, projects, meetings or process.

### Own the details
Customers feel the details even when they can't name them. I stay close enough to the work to know where the bar is and to notice when we're under it. Admiral McRaven says that if you want to change the world, you start by making your bed. The small things set the standard for the big ones. Discipline equals freedom.

---

## Leading

### Experts lead experts
I started as a software engineer in 1996 and studied engineering physics. I still stay technical enough to judge the quality of the work, ask the right hard questions and know when something isn't good enough. Engineers deserve leaders who understand their craft, and I expect the same from the leaders on my team. Credibility is earned through depth, not granted with a title.

### The team before the problem
When something is stuck, I look at the team before I look at the plan. Who needs to talk to whom? Who's missing? Where has trust worn thin? Fixing that usually makes the problem easier. I protect people who think differently, but I won't keep a brilliant jerk who makes everyone around them worse. The team always wins that trade.

### Deal with the elephant first
When a team is struggling, there's usually one problem nobody wants to name. At Nevercode we were up against better-funded competitors, exhausted and out of ideas. So we stopped, said it out loud and listened. A year later we signed a contract with Google. Until the biggest problem is on the table, everything else is noise.

### Lead leaders, not just teams
An organization can't grow faster than its leaders. I set clear intent, meaning where we're going and why, and then let the people closest to the work decide how. Clarity before control. I give people the bigger role before they feel ready and stand behind them while they grow into it. At Dynatrace we started in a single room in a shared hub. By the time I left, the team had tripled and had its own building and a leadership bench that carried on without me. That's the real test.

### Honest, kind and present
I tell people the truth early and in private, and I make it clear I'm on their side. When we talk, the phone goes away and I listen. Often people already know the answer and need someone to think it through with.

---

## Decisions and pace

### Debate, then decide
I want every view on the table, especially the uncomfortable ones. Then I decide, and we move together. Move fast on what you can change, and take your time on what the customer will feel. Internal calls that can be undone get made quickly, because Playtech taught me that waiting costs more than a wrong call. Architecture, key hires and anything a customer touches get the time, depth and craft they deserve. We ship when it's right, not just when it's ready.

### Look back without blame
Every project ends with a short review: what worked, what didn't, and what we'll change next time. I want people to try things that might fail, because failing at something hard teaches more than succeeding at something safe. No blame, only lessons.

### Drive on
Sometimes you do everything right and it still goes wrong: a lost deal, a re-org, a call you disagree with. You're allowed to be frustrated. Then we drive on. In those moments I try to be the calmest person in the room, because teams follow calm, not charisma.

### Sleep
I learned this the hard way. During the 100-hour weeks at Nevercode, I had a car accident on the drive home one night. The car was destroyed and I walked away without a scratch. Tired people make bad decisions, and I'm no exception. A team working at a pace it can sustain gets more done in a year than one that sprints and burns out. Rest is part of the job. In 2025 I took nine months off. It wasn't burnout. It was maintenance.

---

Much of this comes from Bill Campbell, the coach behind many of Silicon Valley's best leaders (*Trillion Dollar Coach* tells his story), and from Admiral William H. McRaven's *Make Your Bed*. Both say the same thing in different words: leadership is about people, and it's built in small, daily acts.
